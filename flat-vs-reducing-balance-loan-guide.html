Flat Rate vs Reducing Balance

Same interest rate, very different cost — here's why
💡 The Short Version
A "6% flat rate" loan can cost you nearly double what a "6% reducing balance" loan costs — for the exact same advertised rate.
The difference is entirely in how the interest is calculated, not the rate itself.

The Two Methods, Explained Simply

Reducing balance (also called declining balance) interest is recalculated every month on whatever principal you still actually owe. As you pay the loan down, the outstanding balance shrinks — so the interest charged each month shrinks too. This is how most bank mortgages and many personal loans work.

Flat rate interest is calculated once, upfront, on the full original loan amount — and that same interest amount gets charged for every single period of the loan term, even in month 59 of a 60-month loan when you've already paid off almost everything. The lender keeps charging interest as if you still owed the full original sum the whole time.

💡 This is why the "same" interest rate can produce two very different bills — the rate is just one input; how it's applied to a shrinking vs. fixed balance is what actually determines the total interest you pay.

A Real Worked Example

Take a 10,000 loan at a 6% annual rate over 5 years (60 months) — the exact same rate, applied the two different ways:

Why This Trips People Up

A lender advertising "6% flat" and a lender advertising "6% reducing balance" are not offering the same deal, even though the headline number looks identical. The flat-rate offer's true (effective) cost is meaningfully higher — for a multi-year term, often somewhere in the range of roughly double the nominal rate, though the exact multiple depends on the loan's length.

⚠️ When comparing loan offers, never compare the headline rate alone. Ask each lender directly whether their quoted rate is flat or reducing balance, and compute the actual monthly payment and total repayment for both using the same calculator — that's the only number that tells you what you'll really pay.

Which Method Do Lenders Actually Use?

It varies by market and product, so always ask rather than assume. In the UAE, banks commonly advertise personal and car loans using a flat rate — it's the market default because the headline number looks smaller, even though a reducing-balance loan is often the better deal for the same advertised rate. Mortgages are typically reducing-balance by nature everywhere, since that's how standard amortization works. In Saudi Arabia, most retail personal financing is structured as Sharia-compliant Murabaha or Tawarruq — priced as a "profit rate" rather than conventional interest — so the flat-vs-reducing-balance framing here doesn't map onto those products the same way; check with your bank how their specific financing structure is priced.

FAQ

What is the difference between flat rate and reducing balance interest?

Flat rate interest is calculated once on the full original loan amount for the entire term, even as you pay down the principal. Reducing balance (also called declining balance) interest is recalculated each period only on whatever principal is still outstanding, so the interest charged shrinks every month as you pay the loan down.

Why does a flat rate loan cost more than a reducing balance loan at the same nominal rate?

Because flat rate interest keeps charging you as if you still owed the full original amount for the whole term, even after you've paid a large part of it off. Reducing balance only charges interest on what you actually still owe. At the same advertised annual rate, a flat rate loan's true cost (effective APR) can end up roughly double the nominal rate.

How do I know which type my loan uses?

Ask your bank or lender directly and check your loan offer document — it should state the calculation method explicitly. If a lender only advertises a flat rate without clarifying, ask for the reducing-balance equivalent (sometimes called the effective or true APR) so you can compare it fairly against other offers.

Can I convert a flat rate to an equivalent reducing balance rate?

Roughly, yes, though the exact conversion depends on the loan term. As a rule of thumb, a flat rate is often quoted as looking close to half its true reducing-balance-equivalent cost for typical multi-year terms — but the only reliable way to compare two real offers is to compute the actual monthly payment and total repayment for both, which is exactly what a loan calculator that supports both modes does.
